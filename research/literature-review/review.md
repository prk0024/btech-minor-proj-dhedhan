## Publication decision

**Reframe the paper before running the larger benchmark.** There is published precedent for summing correlated features to stabilize explanations, for measuring borrower-level rank stability across refits, and for high full-ranking agreement coexisting with unstable shortlists. A mortgage preprint posted on 20 November 2026 also measures changes to an applicant's leading reason, compares individual and grouped reasons, and tests whether a small lead over the runner-up predicts those changes. I would present the existing UCI pilot as a replication with an exploratory extension. I would decline to submit it as a novel algorithm or an original discovery of reason instability.

**The remaining research opportunity is narrower.** Can the opposing monthly contributions inside a coherent credit-history group help an analyst identify, *before retraining*, which group explanations will change on a future refit? How much does inspecting those components add beyond the lead over the runner-up and the strength of the group's net contribution? Among the methods inspected, I did not identify a paper evaluating this exact incremental, held-out comparison in repeated credit histories. Its novelty is provisional. Its usefulness is an experimental question. The output of interest is a model-audit diagnostic with a measurable benefit or a well-supported negative result.

## What the current experiment establishes

The UCI outcome is next-month credit-card default. There are 30,000 accounts, one stratified split, 500 held-out audit accounts, ten logistic-regression bootstrap fits, and 30 size-matched random feature partitions. The explanations are signed contributions to default log-odds against a training-data background. For each group the pilot sums contributions for each account **before** taking absolute values and averaging across accounts. The global ranking therefore answers a different question from the ranking for one person. [UCI dataset](https://doi.org/10.24432/C55S3H) · [Archived implementation](https://github.com/prk0024/btech-minor-proj-dhedhan/blob/e620c9f76ccacb6f0af78300e1fac5dee4ea4f00/demo.py)

| Agreement across refits | Correlation groups | Size-matched random groups |
|---|---:|---:|
| Global ranking, rank-biased overlap | 0.9240 | 0.8631 |
| Global first place, pair agreement | 100.00% | 84.59% |
| Per-account ranking, mean rank-biased overlap | 0.8525 | 0.8261 |
| Per-account first place, pair agreement | 78.49% | 80.55% |

The first row reproduces the archived pilot. Rows two through four and the cancellation analysis come from the local exploratory replay. The 5,000 account–fit records reuse 500 people. The 45 fit pairs reuse ten models. The original ranking advantage and the later first-place reversal are descriptive. Rankings within each random partition use the same feature grouping across all fits; the meaning of a random group changes between partitions. Agreement on an arbitrary group can be high even if its label would be unhelpful to a reader. [Archived pilot](https://github.com/prk0024/btech-minor-proj-dhedhan/blob/e620c9f76ccacb6f0af78300e1fac5dee4ea4f00/results/pilot.json)

For six monthly bill features, the median fraction cancelled was 85.6% across account–fit records. The mean unsigned bill contribution was 0.681 log-odds and the mean absolute net contribution was 0.101. A high cancellation fraction can be the *correct* representation of a model that relies on changes between months. The pilot also ranks by **absolute** contribution: a group lowering default risk can come first. Describing that group as a reason for denial would change both the target and the meaning of the explanation.

## The closest prior work

**1. Laberge et al., JMLR, 2023 — the closest methodological precedent.** In *Partial Order in Chaos*, the authors study local and global explanations across similarly performing models. In their housing study they observe trade-offs between correlated inputs. Equation 30 explicitly sums their two local attributions. Figures 3–5 compare grouped and individual explanations; the sum can acquire a consistent sign across models even when its members individually lack sign consensus. The overlap with the mechanism used in COMET is direct. Their target is consensus within a set of good models. COMET could instead estimate how much the composition of a group helps predict loss of stability on future training resamples. **Evidence:** journal full text, Eq. 30 and Figs. 3–5. [Paper](https://jmlr.org/papers/v24/23-0149.html)

**2. Khan, SSRN, 2026 — the closest application precedent.** *Do Adverse-Action Reasons Survive a Retrain?* studies US mortgage decisions over 2018–2024. The author reports 256,139 denied applicants explained. Across the selected similarly accurate models, 31.7% of applicants had at least one change in their top input. The pairwise change probability was 10.7%. After contributions were summed into notice-statement groups, the corresponding figures were 31.0% and 10.0%. The study also measures top-four overlap, annual refit effects and disagreement by the lead over the second-ranked reason. It defines the principal input using a **positive contribution toward denial**. COMET’s first-place percentages use a different target, denominator and model population. The useful baseline for a new study is Khan’s top-two margin. **Evidence:** SSRN posting and author’s submission manuscript, sections 4.4 and 5.2, Tables 3, 5 and 6; public research code inspected as a source, results not independently reproduced. This is a preprint. [SSRN](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7721099) · [Author manuscript and code](https://github.com/ayk5511/reasonstab/tree/98e9a6384e0d847edc756a64f97547f2c24afaa8/paper)

**3. Ballegeer, Bogaert and Benoit, EJOR, 2025 — the closest peer-reviewed credit benchmark.** The study measures SHAP and LIME stability for individual credit applicants under controlled resampling, varying class imbalance and cost-sensitive model training across four credit datasets. Section 3.6 defines a coefficient of variation and sequential rank agreement, with rank depth ten in the experiments. Credit-domain refits, local ranking stability and top-weighted evaluation all have direct prior art. Their main comparison concerns training objectives and imbalance. The monthly composition of a signed group is a different experimental factor. **Evidence:** accepted manuscript, Table 2, section 3.6 and results. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2025.05.039) · [Open manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01409)

**4. Rahman, Next Materials, 2026 — the closest precedent for the headline.** *Stable rankings, unstable shortlists* compares global SHAP rankings for materials prediction across 45 fitted models. Within algorithms it reports mean full-ranking Spearman correlations of 0.88–0.96 and top-ten overlaps of 0.60–0.65. This directly anticipates the argument that a stable full ranking can conceal a changing shortlist. Its units are global descriptors and its shortlists have ten entries. A borrower-specific first-place experiment addresses a narrower question. **Evidence:** indexed abstract, bibliographic record and author-deposited results JSON; full article methods were unavailable in this review. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nxmate.2026.103038) · [Supporting data and code](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20839498)

**5. Jullum, Redelmeier and Aas — the attribution definition.** *groupShapley* covers feature-type and dependence-based groups, compares merging players before computing Shapley values with summing feature values afterward, and studies conditions under which those constructions agree. For the pilot’s linear logit and interventional background, the signed sum reconstructs the block’s additive contribution. For a tree with interactions the interpretation needs its own check. **Evidence:** full paper, sections 2–3 and simulations. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12228)

**6. Hendro, Mei and Welch, ITNAC, 2025 — robustness after grouping.** The authors sum one-hot inputs into semantic groups, run random forests and boosted trees across 30 seeds, and assess faithfulness, complexity and sensitivity to nearby inputs. Their group construction restores original categorical variables. Their sensitivity measure compares nearby observations, whereas COMET compares explanations of the same account under refitted models. This paper rules out claiming that evaluating a robustness–information trade-off after grouping is new. **Evidence:** accepted manuscript, methodology, Fig. 1 and Eq. 5. [Conference article](https://doi.org/10.1109/ITNAC66378.2025.11302604) · [Open manuscript](https://doi.org/10.25455/wgtn.31071808)

**7. Kim and Park, CIKM, 2026 — temporal groups and a random-group control.** *GroupSegment-SHAP* builds variable groups using training-data dependence and segments time; it evaluates deletion-based faithfulness and synthetic ground-truth recovery. Section 4.3 compares dependence grouping, Pearson-correlation grouping, individual features and random grouping with the same **number** of groups. The manuscript specifies this count match; it does not establish from that passage that each group size was matched. COMET preserves the exact list of group sizes and scores retraining agreement. That is a useful design choice. It would be a weak standalone novelty claim. **Evidence:** conference-linked manuscript, sections 3 and 4.3, Table 5 and Appendix E. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1145/3799682.3840928) · [Open manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06114)

## Supporting literature and its implications

- **Metric choice changes the conclusion.** Krishna et al. distinguish feature, rank, sign and signed-rank agreement on top-k explanations, alongside rank correlations. Kamp et al. show in text classification that choosing a dynamic rather than fixed shortlist changes agreement comparisons. Goldwasser and Hooker verify top-k sets and orders under attribution-estimation noise. Their inference concerns a different source of randomness from training resamples. COMET should distinguish these sources explicitly. **Evidence:** full manuscripts, including Krishna section 3.2. [Krishna et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01602) · [Kamp et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.379/) · [Goldwasser and Hooker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15800)
- **Grouping is already a substantial literature.** Au et al. compare grouped permutation, refitting and Shapley-based importance; Huber et al. interpret grouped feature contributions in yield prediction. Grouped SHAP-style inference has also been applied in finance to bond recovery, with 98 features organized into 16 subgroups and five broad categories. These are useful comparison methods and examples of domain-defined groups. Financial applications and meaningful grouped visualizations have ample precedent. **Evidence:** full papers or author manuscripts. [Au et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-022-00840-5) · [Huber et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07111) · [Bond recovery study](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-08451-x)
- **Agreement depends on what is being held fixed.** Ley et al. study inconsistent explanations under model indeterminacy and ensembling. The Feature Attribution Stability Suite conditions on prediction preservation under *input perturbations* and contrasts rank correlation with top-k overlap. Parashar et al. study agreement across explanation methods and regional versus global comparisons. COMET can hold borrower, background convention and explainer fixed, while varying only the training sample, then report separately how much predictions moved. **Evidence:** full manuscripts; for Parashar the inspected full text was the earlier preprint version. [Ley et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06193) · [Stability suite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02532) · [Parashar et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12065-026-01145-y)
- **The word “cancellation” also has prior art.** Ellenberg studies signed attributions and unsigned magnitudes, synthetic cancellation and collinearity, and bootstrap refitting in a modular medical predictor. Its target is the routing of attribution magnitude through a graph. This is a concrete reason to avoid claiming an original cancellation statistic. Ibebuchi combines bootstrap rank stability, direction and predictor-correlation uncertainty in scientific ML. **Evidence:** published or indexed abstracts for these two studies; their source code and detailed estimands were not audited. [Ellenberg](https://doi.org/10.1111/exsy.70462) · [Ibebuchi](https://doi.org/10.1088/2632-2153/ae8a13)
- **Attribution targets and data provenance matter.** SHAP defines additive attribution relative to an explanation game. Dependence-aware attribution and interventional attribution answer different questions. Historical monthly bills are observations recorded before default; variation in their fitted coefficients can reveal model behavior without identifying an intervention a borrower should take. The original UCI data are a default-prediction benchmark. [Lundberg and Lee](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07874) · [Aas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.10464) · [Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16234) · [Dataset](https://doi.org/10.24432/C55S3H)

## The experiment I would fund

**Primary question.** For a fixed reference model and an unseen borrower, does the internal composition of the leading group predict a subsequent winner change beyond the reference model’s top-two margin? Call the magnitude-based winner the *largest contributor*. For a directional secondary analysis, separately rank only contributions that raise estimated default risk and retain an explicit case for no positive group. UCI cannot establish which reason a lender gave for a credit decision.

1. **Specify the outcome and competitors before opening a new test result.** Given a reference model, fixed groups and one borrower, estimate the fraction of independently refitted comparators that give a different winner. Compare a margin-only forecast with a forecast using margin, reference net strength, group identity/size and risk score. Then add within-group composition. Evaluate paired change in Brier score and calibration on held-out borrowers and refits. Count eligible people, ties, zero-mass groups and changed predictions. Report a sensitivity restricted to refits with similar predicted risk. Reserve the already-inspected split for exploration.
2. **Handle the algebra explicitly.** If the signed contributions are $a_j$, let $S=\sum_j a_j$, $U=\sum_j |a_j|$ and $C=1-|S|/U$ for $U>0$. $C$ is fully determined by $|S|$ and $U$. It cannot provide independent information beyond those two quantities jointly. Compare its predictive performance with the cheaper margin-only rule and with a baseline using net and unsigned strength. Evaluate large signs on opposite months, their locations, or level-versus-change patterns if a genuine composition effect is proposed. Record undefined cases, signed direction and scale; a near-zero net alone is insufficient evidence of an important hidden effect.
3. **Test the mechanism and the easy alternatives.** Use native monthly coordinates, train-only clusters, exact size-matched random partitions and prespecified month blocks a credit analyst could name. Compare each real partition with controls of the same sizes. Report top-group frequencies because a partition with one dominant group can win agreement trivially. On synthetic data, hold proxy correlations similar while switching the known model between average bill level and month-to-month change. A difference model can legitimately show strong cancellation. For a nonlinear model compare summed attributions with exact small-group game values where tractable.
4. **Use independent evidence.** Freeze the protocol, compare logit and one tree model, run multiple prespecified splits, and obtain a second suitably licensed benchmark with comparable repeated measurements before claiming portability across credit histories. Repeated splits from UCI reuse people. Separate conditional borrower uncertainty from training-sample uncertainty; do not treat the 45 pairs or the 5,000 account–fit rows as independent samples. Prefer per-setting effects with uncertainty to a single pooled significance test.
5. **Decide what result merits a paper.** A useful positive result is reproducible, out-of-sample improvement over the margin baseline, with interpretable failure cases, adequate support and acceptable calibration. A useful negative result is a sufficiently precise demonstration that the margin explains most apparent instability or that simple month blocks match learned groups across relevant settings. If only the original split is positive or a difference vanishes under a fair control, finish the minor-project report and scope any workshop paper as a replication. Establish venue fit, current call, and any artifact requirements only after the contribution is clear.

**Positioning sentence for a proposal:** “We will evaluate whether a borrower’s pattern of offsetting monthly default-risk contributions helps predict instability of a grouped explanation under model refitting, beyond the lead of the highest-ranked group and its net strength.” Use future tense until the held-out tests exist. A study applying an existing method can earn publication through evidence that changes what readers measure or do; the current data establish neither that incremental benefit nor acceptance by any venue.

## Search scope, traceability and limits

The review used the project’s dossier as a seed and performed a new date-bounded search in OpenAlex and Crossref through 22 November 2026. It included journal and conference work, preprints, open author manuscripts and companion artifacts. Retrieval used 24 topic queries in both indexes, up to 25 relevance-ranked results for each query–index pair. The fixed queries span grouped and correlated attributions, credit decisions, model multiplicity, local/global rankings, shortlist uncertainty, temporal aggregation and signed cancellation. The query wording accompanies every search hit in the ledger. Three additional phrase searches in OpenAlex returned 15 retained hits each. Exact-title lookups, DOI checks, author-repository searches, and references inside full texts supplemented ranked retrieval.

For citation chaining I queried indexed references and citing papers for nine seed records: Ballegeer, Hendro, Jullum, Krishna, Parashar, Khan, Kim and Park, Rahman, and Goldwasser and Hooker. The ledger labels them respectively as credit, itnac, group, disagree, consensus, mortgage, groupsegment, rahman, and rank. All available forward pages for those identifier records were requested. Recently deposited papers and separate preprint/journal records may have incomplete graphs. A zero indexed citing count carries little weight.

Mechanical deduplication by lower-case DOI (normalized title when DOI absent) yields **1,790 keys**. Preprint and journal versions can retain separate keys; targeted direct lookups lie outside those counts. The ledger is a retrieval trail, including irrelevant hits. Its size is **not** the number of papers read in full. Detailed assessment prioritized papers with direct overlap on the target, grouping, ranking depth, training perturbation, comparison partitions or cancellation. Attributions of missing methods are bounded by the sections and versions inspected. For the closest mortgage preprint the SSRN posting was verified against the downloadable submission manuscript in the author’s repository; for Rahman the abstract and public supplemental results were accessible. No third-party full-text corpus is redistributed.

This was a single-reviewer, targeted scoping review using public indexes. There was no independent duplicate screening, exhaustive search of subscription indexes or survey of proprietary lender research. The targeted searches supplement, rather than establish coverage of, unindexed work or different terminology. A universal priority claim and a publication guarantee would exceed the evidence. The specific overlap findings above are sufficient for the decision to change direction regardless of any further omissions.
